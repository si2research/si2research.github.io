Stories / MIT Science Hackathon, November 2026

Qatalyst: can a swarm of AI agents turn an abstract into a quantum experiment?

We gave AI agents nothing but a paper’s abstract and asked them to rebuild the algorithm: equations, Qiskit code, and a circuit that runs in simulation. Then we let a team of agents compete for survival on a shared ledger to see whether organization beats raw intelligence.

Sanjay Das, Ethan Seefried, Chris Kellogg, Tahisha Jain, Nafiza Shazia Aviedi
8 min read

Code on GitHub Read the full report See generated circuits

The short version

  • The task: rebuild a quantum algorithm from its abstract alone, as equations, Qiskit code, and a simulated circuit. Benchmark of 96 papers from a 300-paper corpus; full texts stay sealed for scoring.
  • One strong agent recovers the most math. Single-agent Sonnet matched 54 of 320 key paper equations for $7.52 in total.
  • A searching swarm builds the most working circuits. Our Abstract2Method swarm got 93 of 96 papers to simulation, versus 77 for the single agent, at about 8× the cost.
  • The survival-game swarm is inspectable end to end. In a partial batch, our hybrid Nemotron + Sonnet swarm reached a reviewed, executing circuit on 15 of 21 papers, at a median 156 seconds and about $0.29 per paper.

The missing middle between an idea and an experiment

An abstract is a promise in miniature. It says what a method achieves, but leaves out almost everything you need to test it: the equations, the registers, the sequence of gates, the measurement.

Closing that gap is slow, expert work, and it is where much of science stalls. Reproducing a result, checking a claim, or adapting a method to a new problem all begin with the same step: turning a description into something that runs.

Qatalyst asks how much of that step AI agents can do, and, more interestingly, how agents should be organized to do it well. Quantum computing is an ideal testbed: circuits are small, simulation is exact, and the software ecosystem is fragmented enough that reconstruction is genuinely hard.

Four ways to organize an AI research team

Think of rebuilding a dish from a menu description. One cook can do everything. A head chef can split the work among assistants. Or the cooks can pick tasks from a shared order board, build on each other’s attempts, and lose their station if they keep burning the food. AI systems face the same choice, and we built and tested all of them.

A ledger-based survival game

Every agent in the hybrid swarm starts with 5 units of virtual energy. Proposing, implementing, and testing all cost energy. An agent earns energy back only when one of its candidates executes in the simulator and passes review by an agent outside its own implementation chain. Rewards for progress are deferred until both checks pass, so agents can’t get paid for confident-sounding guesses.

When an agent hits zero, it is culled. A spawner looks at the ledger’s history, clones the role preferences and decoding strategy of a successful agent, mutates one decoding parameter, and drops the replacement back into the team. If nothing has succeeded yet, the replacement explores a fresh strategy instead. Energy is search bookkeeping, separate from API spending and from software crashes.

Inside one attempt

The hybrid swarm splits the work between two kinds of model. A fine-tuned NVIDIA Nemotron Bolt model, served locally on a GPU, proposes numbered LaTeX equations from the abstract. Claude Sonnet plans methods, assembles the mathematical specification, writes the Qiskit code, and reviews results. Two agents can join implementation chains; the third is held back as an independent reviewer.

Generated code never runs on the host. It is built inside a locked-down Docker worker with no network access and simulated with Qiskit Aer. Build errors, simulation output, and review comments flow back to the ledger and shape the next revision. The paper’s full text and its hand-labelled key equations stay sealed and are used only for scoring.

What success and failure look like

Three runs from the hybrid swarm show the range of outcomes. Each one leaves a full trail on the ledger: proposals, code, execution, criticism, and any agents that died along the way.

Recovered, with functional checks

Affine function identification

The abstract describes identifying a hidden Boolean function f(x) = a·x ⊕ b. The swarm explored several approaches and chose a circuit that combines an offset readout with a Bernstein–Vazirani step. An excerpt of the code it wrote:

# plain U_f: Z on q_i for a_i=1, global phase (-1)^b
for i in range(n):
    if bits[i] == 1:
        qc.z(data[i])
if b == 1:
    qc.global_phase += math.pi
for i in range(n):
    qc.h(data[i])

n=2, a=01, b=1 → measured 101 in 1,024 of 1,024 shots

Independent checks recovered both a and b in 28 of 28 small instances, one to three data qubits. The run took about 4.4 minutes and $0.31. Scope matters: the circuit takes the oracle parameters as explicit inputs and assumes phase-oracle access, so it does not establish the paper’s query-complexity claims. Sonnet also rejected all eight native equation proposals and assembled the winning specification itself.

Ran, but not approved

Order finding with imperfect gates

For an N = 15 instance, the swarm produced a circuit that built and simulated. But with default all-zero input, the output piled up on 00000000, and the reviewer asked for the work register’s initialization to be made consistent across the specification, circuit, and runner.

Repairs followed. Two agents ran out of energy and were replaced, and the run hit its time limit after 303 seconds without an approved candidate.

We report this one because it shows what the review stage is for: a circuit that runs is not the same as a circuit that is right. The reviewer’s objection was not confirmed with a numerical counterexample, so this remains an open reconstruction rather than a certified failure.

Results: teams help with some things, not everything

We ran three Sonnet-based systems on the same 96-paper benchmark, with the paper equations hidden. The math score measures how closely an agent’s equations match the paper’s labelled key equations, adjusted for chance. Simulated means the generated circuit built and ran in Aer, which is necessary but not sufficient for correctness.

The survival-game hybrid, partial batch

The newer hybrid swarm adds the local Nemotron equation model and the energy economy. At our snapshot, 21 of a planned 65 papers had finished.

On the same 21 papers, Nemotron alone, used as a single generator, got 6 circuits to simulation. The comparison is descriptive, not causal: the hybrid adds Sonnet assembly, review, and a bigger budget. And none of the 15 selected candidates kept a Nemotron equation verbatim, so the local model’s own contribution is not yet established.

What we take away

  1. Organization changes what you get, not just how much. A single agent is the most efficient way to recover the paper’s mathematics. A swarm that explores several method families and tests them against a simulator is the most reliable way to get something that runs.
  2. How a swarm is organized matters more than having one. On the same model, the Abstract2Method swarm beat the contract-splitting ledger swarm on every quality measure, mainly by exploring multiple methods per paper instead of dividing one.
  3. Running and approved is still not correct. Execution and model review filter out a lot, but only explicit functional checks, like the 28-instance affine test, show that an algorithm does what it claims.

Domain transfer: from quantum circuits to materials science

Can the same abstract-to-experiment loop work for atomic structures? We tested it on an unseen basin-hopping paper about Lennard-Jones clusters: finding low-energy arrangements of atoms by turning a rugged energy landscape into a staircase of local minima. Agents received the abstract and a Python execution contract; the full paper and hidden checks stayed with the evaluator.

A sequential agent and a three-agent decentralized swarm used the same fine-tuned Nemotron Bolt checkpoint, seed, tests, and search-budget ceilings. Each used 10 model calls and about 26,000 search tokens on one GPU with serialized generation. The swarm recorded three energy deaths and three replacements, but its initial agents proposed identical reconstructions.

Both systems passed 0 of 3 public-plus-hidden cases: a Python NameError stopped execution before usable atomic coordinates were returned. The result locates an implementation/runtime integration gap; physical-method accuracy remains unestablished. This one-paper, one-seed pilot shows how the workflow can be exercised in another domain, not successful scientific transfer or a swarm advantage. The next step is to repair the interface, rerun independent numerical checks, and broaden the evaluation.

Materials pilot snapshot: November 1, 2026, 12:42 p.m. EST. Shared search ceilings: 10 model calls, 90,000 tokens, 840 seconds, and 12 simulations. Run order was not randomized; timings do not measure distributed speedup.

Why this matters

Our mission at SI² is to connect scientific reasoning with experimentation, so ideas turn into evidence faster. Qatalyst is a first step: a loop in which an idea becomes a candidate, the candidate meets a test, and the evidence shapes the next attempt, with every step on the record.

Reconstruction is the training ground. A paper’s abstract is a claim with a known answer. An open problem is a claim whose answer nobody has written down yet. If agents can reliably rebuild known methods and prove they work, the same loop can be pointed at problems that are still open.

What comes next

Next: trust

Checks that rerun, not just read

Replace model approval with independent functional contracts on every paper, and a quorum of verifiers that re-execute candidates. Complete the 96- and 65-paper batches, repeat with budget-matched seeds, and run the Nemotron ablation.

Then: breadth

Beyond quantum circuits

Build on the initial materials pilot with broader numerical checks, explore chemistry and materials simulation with NVIDIA ALCHEMI, and extend to protein modelling using simulators and public benchmarks.

Goal: discovery

From known claims to open ones

Write claim cards for open problems, such as a compiler pass that beats today’s best on standard circuits. With no paper to match, success means beating the known baseline in simulation and surviving expert review.
